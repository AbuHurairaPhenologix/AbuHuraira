# ThermoTwin.NET: Mathematical Modeling, Numerical Simulation, Inverse Reconstruction and PDE-Constrained Optimal Control of Battery Thermal Systems

*Technical report accompanying the ThermoTwin.NET repository. All quantitative statements are reproduced by `dotnet run -c Release --project src/backend/ThermoTwin.Cli -- docs/results` and can be checked in [`docs/results/summary.md`](results/summary.md). Derivations are in [mathematics.md](mathematics.md); experimental protocols in [experiments.md](experiments.md).*

## Abstract

We model the in-plane temperature of a lithium-ion pouch cell during fast charging by a linear parabolic PDE with a bilinear cold-plate control term. The model is discretised twice and independently: by cell-centred finite volumes and by Galerkin P1 finite elements, both with Crank–Nicolson time stepping. The two are verified against exact and manufactured solutions: second order in L² for both, first order in H¹ for the finite elements. They agree with each other to 4.8·10⁻⁴ K at the finest resolution.

From twelve noisy point sensors we reconstruct an unknown, spatially localised heat source by Tikhonov regularisation with generalised cross-validation. This gives a full-field temperature estimate with 0.053 K RMSE and a hotspot location error of 2.3 mm on synthetic data generated by a finer, independent plant model. A local sensitivity and Fisher-information analysis shows that the defect parameters are practically identifiable jointly with conductivity and edge cooling, with condition number 6.75 and largest collinearity index 4.45. The analysis also shows that fixing the latter at wrong values biases the defect amplitude by 7.9 %.

We then formulate cooling as a PDE-constrained optimal-control problem with a pointwise state constraint, relaxed by a Moreau–Yosida penalty. The gradient is computed with an exact discrete adjoint; it agrees with central finite differences to 1.2·10⁻⁸ and costs ≈ 31 ms regardless of the number of controls. Finite differences cost 1.6 s for 90 controls. With the same algorithm the adjoint optimiser is 4.3× faster than its finite-difference counterpart and reaches a KKT residual of 4.8·10⁻⁵. The resulting plan uses 45 % less pump energy than the cheapest safe constant cooling.

Finally, a POD–Galerkin reduced model with 40 modes reproduces held-out full-order trajectories to 0.023 K RMSE. Embedded in a certified optimisation loop, it makes model-predictive control 8.5× faster per decision than full-order adjoint MPC while keeping the ground-truth plant below its safety limit. All results are synthetic benchmarks; no validation against measured battery data has been performed.

## 1. Problem definition

During fast charging, Joule heating raises the temperature of a battery cell. A local defect (a degraded tab weld, lithium plating, a high-resistance region) adds heat in a small area and can drive the cell towards thermal runaway before surface sensors notice. A battery-management system has a few thermistors, measures the current, and controls a coolant pump. ThermoTwin addresses four coupled mathematical questions:

1. **Forward problem.** Given a heat source and a cooling schedule, how does the temperature field evolve?
2. **Inverse problem.** Given sparse, noisy temperature histories, which heat source, and hence which full temperature field, explains them?
3. **Model reduction.** Can the forward model be replaced by a low-dimensional surrogate that is fast enough for repeated optimisation, without losing safety?
4. **Optimal control.** What is the cheapest cooling schedule that keeps T(**x**, t) ≤ T<sub>safe</sub> everywhere?

## 2. Physical assumptions

- **Thin cell.** The thickness δ = 10 mm is small against 200 × 100 mm, so the temperature is depth-averaged and the face-mounted cold plate becomes a volumetric sink −(h(u)/δ)(T − T<sub>c</sub>).
- **Homogenised, isotropic, constant properties.** ρc<sub>p</sub> = 2.5 MJ/(m³K), in-plane k = 20 W/(mK). Real cells are anisotropic and temperature-dependent.
- **Resistive heat only.** q = s(t)q̂(**x**) with s(t) = (I/I<sub>ref</sub>)² known. Entropic and reaction heat are neglected, and the defect does not evolve in time.
- **Convective edges** (h<sub>e</sub> = 10 W/(m²K)) and a coolant at 22 °C. The cold-plate coefficient is affine in the pump level, h(u) = 5 + 115u W/(m²K), and the pump power is cubic, P(u) = 150u³ W.

## 3. Governing mathematical model

On Ω = (0, 0.2) × (0, 0.1) m:

$$
\rho c_p\,\partial_t T - \nabla\cdot(k\nabla T) + \frac{h(u)}{\delta}(T - T_c) = s(t)\,\hat q(\mathbf x), \qquad t \in (0, \tau].
$$

The equation is linear, second-order and parabolic. The control enters bilinearly, so the control-to-state map is nonlinear but smooth. With α = k/ρc<sub>p</sub> = 8·10⁻⁶ m²/s, the diffusion time across the short side is τ<sub>d</sub> = L<sub>y</sub>²/α = 1250 s, and the Fourier number of the 30-minute charge is Fo = 1.44. The defect's heat therefore reaches the sensors during one charge, but in a smoothed form. The edge Biot number Bi<sub>e</sub> = 0.05 is small, while the cooling number Γ(u) = h(u)L<sub>y</sub>²/(δk) ranges over 0.25–6. The cold plate dominates heat removal and is a meaningful control.

## 4. Boundary and initial conditions

Robin conditions −k∂T/∂n = h<sub>e</sub>(T − T<sub>∞</sub>) on all edges, and T(**x**, 0) = 25 °C. Dirichlet and Neumann conditions are implemented for verification. The weak formulation (mathematics §5) shows that the bilinear form is coercive on H¹(Ω) thanks to the sink term alone. Every implicit time step is therefore uniquely solvable (Lax–Milgram), and an energy estimate bounds the temperature excess by the source magnitude divided by the cooling rate.

## 5. Finite-volume method

A cell-centred grid with a five-point flux balance gives ∇²T ≈ AT + b. Ghost cells close all three boundary types; the Robin closure interpolates exactly between Dirichlet and insulated edges. A is symmetric negative definite with half-bandwidth N<sub>y</sub> under short-side-first ordering. The scheme is exactly conservative: the relative energy imbalance on an insulated cell is below 10⁻¹³ for all time schemes.

## 6. Finite-element weak formulation

Testing with v ∈ H¹(Ω) and integrating the diffusion term by parts gives

$$
\int \rho c_p\,\partial_t T\,v + \int k\nabla T\cdot\nabla v + \int \tfrac{h(u)}{\delta} T v + \oint h_e T v = \int q v + \int \tfrac{h(u)}{\delta}T_c v + \oint h_e T_\infty v .
$$

With continuous piecewise-linear functions on the triangulated grid (each FV cell split into two triangles), this yields ρc<sub>p</sub>MṪ + (K + R + H(u)M)T = Mq + H(u)T<sub>c</sub>M𝟙 + r. The element mass and stiffness matrices are computed exactly from the affine map and the constant barycentric gradients. Dirichlet nodes are eliminated symmetrically, so the system stays SPD for banded Cholesky. At 40×20 the FEM has 861 unknowns and 5 781 non-zeros (half-bandwidth 22); the FVM has 800 and 3 880 (half-bandwidth 20).

## 7. Temporal discretisation

Both semi-discrete systems are integrated with the θ-method. Crank–Nicolson (θ = ½) is second-order and A-stable but not L-stable. Implicit Euler is first-order and L-stable. Explicit Euler requires Δt ≤ 2/ρ(M); ρ(M) is estimated by power iteration and bounded by Gershgorin. The implicit matrices are factorised once per cooling level and reused across steps.

## 8. Verification and convergence

| Test | Quantity | Observed order (finest pair) | Expected |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Eigenmode, FVM, CN, Δt ∝ h² | RMSE | 2.000 | 2 |
| Eigenmode, FVM, temporal (CN / IE) | RMSE | 2.000 / 0.997 | 2 / 1 |
| Eigenmode, FEM P1 | L² / H¹ | 1.999 / 1.000 | 2 / 1 |
| Manufactured (sink + source), FEM P1 | L² / H¹ | 1.997 / 1.000 | 2 / 1 |
| Manufactured, FVM | RMSE | 2.001 | 2 |
| Battery model, FVM ↔ FEM difference | RMS | 1.99 (ratio 3.99) | 2 |

The explicit scheme is stable at 0.99 Δt<sub>crit</sub> and diverges at 1.01 Δt<sub>crit</sub>. On the realistic battery model, which has no exact solution, the two discretisations converge to a common limit: RMS difference 2.85·10⁻² → 4.76·10⁻⁴ K from 20×10 to 160×80, and peak temperatures 49.333 vs 49.329 °C at the finest grid.

## 9. Sparse observation model

Twelve thermistors on a 6 × 2 lattice read bilinear interpolations of the field, y = CT + ε with ε ~ N(0, (0.1 K)²I), every 5 s. To avoid the *inverse crime*, the synthetic "true" battery runs on a 2× finer grid (80×40) with the exact Gaussian defect (σ = 12 mm, 450 kW/m³ at (138, 64) mm). The defect lies between sensors and is not representable in the estimator's source basis.

## 10. Inverse heat-source reconstruction

The unknown source shape is expanded in 91 bilinear hat functions (16.7 mm spacing). By linearity, T(t) = T<sub>0</sub>(t) + Σ<sub>j</sub>q<sub>j</sub>T<sub>j</sub>(t), so the stacked sensor histories satisfy d = Aq + ε. The basis responses are advanced alongside the data, and AᵀA, Aᵀd, dᵀd are accumulated recursively, so the cost per step is independent of the history length. The forward operator is smoothing, hence compact with rapidly decaying singular values, and naive least squares amplifies the noise into sources of thousands of kW/m³.

## 11. Regularisation

Tikhonov regularisation min‖Aq − d‖² + λ‖Lq‖² with a first-difference prior is solved from the normal equations by dense Cholesky. At the end of the charge (m = 4320, n = 91):

| Rule | T RMSE | Hotspot error | Source error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| GCV | 0.053 K | 2.3 mm | 42.5 % |
| Oracle (minimum source error) | 0.052 K | 2.1 mm | 41.9 % |
| L-curve corner | 0.070 K | 9.0 mm | 45.4 % |
| Discrepancy principle | 5.64 K | 127 mm | 5694 % |

GCV tracks the oracle. The discrepancy principle fails *because of model error*: its target σ√m = 6.57 K lies below the attainable residual of about 6.60 K. This is a known weakness of the principle when the data error is not purely stochastic, and we report it rather than tune it away. The source error remains around 40 % because the smooth basis and smoothing prior spread the 12 mm peak (reconstructed peak about half the true one). The temperature field, the quantity relevant for safety, is nevertheless reconstructed to 0.05 K, because diffusion is insensitive to exactly these unresolved details.

## 12. Parameter sensitivity and identifiability

We asked whether the sensors can separate the defect (amplitude Q, position x<sub>0</sub>, y<sub>0</sub>) from uncertainty in conductivity k and edge cooling h<sub>e</sub>. With central-difference sensitivities scaled by prior uncertainties (20 %, 50 %, 33 %, 10 mm, 10 mm):

- every parameter changes the sensor data by 2.4–16 noise standard deviations;
- the most collinear pair is **h<sub>e</sub> and Q** (cosine −0.90): more edge cooling mimics a weaker defect;
- the column-normalised sensitivity matrix has condition number 6.75 and maximal collinearity index 4.45, below the 10–15 threshold for practical non-identifiability. Cramér–Rao standard deviations are 0.06–1.3 %;
- bounded Levenberg–Marquardt on plant-grid data recovers all five parameters jointly to within 0.94 %. With k and h<sub>e</sub> fixed at mis-specified values, Q is biased by +7.9 % and the location by 2.5 / 3.7 mm, and the noise estimate rises from 0.097 to 0.340 K.

The conclusion is local and model-conditional: under this model, joint estimation is well conditioned, and ignoring parameter uncertainty is the dominant risk.

## 13. Reduced-order modelling

Snapshots of the full model are compressed by proper orthogonal decomposition. The method of snapshots uses a hand-written Householder–QL symmetric eigensolver, cross-checked against Jacobi, and the dynamics are Galerkin-projected. Because the cooling sink is a multiple of the identity, it commutes with the diffusion operator. The reduced operator is therefore diagonalised once, and every cooling level costs O(r) per step. The same property implies that the reachable state space does not depend on the cooling schedule; it does depend on the source shape.

A naive training set of six defect locations captures 1 − 10⁻¹² of its own snapshot energy but generalises poorly: 0.16 K RMSE for a defect elsewhere. This is the Kolmogorov n-width of a moving localised feature. Training instead on the responses to the 91 hat functions of the inverse problem's basis gives 0.023 K RMSE and a 0.094 K peak error at r = 40 on held-out tests, with the ROM error within 2.5 % of the projection error (Galerkin quasi-optimality). Captured energy alone is thus not an adequate model-selection criterion; out-of-sample validation is.

## 14. PDE-constrained optimisation

The control problem is

$$
\min_{u\in[0,1]^{K}}\; \frac{1}{E_{ref}}\sum_k P(u_k)\Delta t_k + w\sum_k (u_{k+1}-u_k)^2
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \text{heat equation},\quad T(\mathbf x,t) \le T_{safe}\ \ \forall(\mathbf x, t).
$$

The pointwise state constraint is relaxed by a Moreau–Yosida penalty μ·mean max(0, T − T<sub>safe</sub>)² with continuation μ = 10² → 10⁵. It is C¹, so gradients exist everywhere, unlike a penalty on max<sub>x</sub>T. Each subproblem is solved by a projected BFGS method (Bertsekas) with Armijo backtracking along the projection arc. A final bisection removes any O(1/μ) residual violation.

## 15. Adjoint method

For the discrete state equation L(u)x<sup>n+1</sup> = R(u)x<sup>n</sup> + Δtĝ<sup>n</sup>(u), the Lagrangian yields the backward adjoint recursion

$$
L(u^{n-1})^\top\lambda^n = R(u^n)^\top\lambda^{n+1} - \mu\,\partial P/\partial x^n,\qquad
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial u_k} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial u_k} + \sum_{n\in k}\sigma'\Delta t\,(\lambda^{n+1})^\top[\theta x^{n+1} + (1-\theta)x^n - d].
$$

Since the diffusion operator is symmetric, Lᵀ = L and Rᵀ = R, and the backward sweep reuses the forward factorisations. This is the exact gradient of the discrete objective. Verification:

- relative error against central finite differences 1.2·10⁻⁸ at ε = 10⁻⁵, with the expected truncation/round-off V-shape;
- the Taylor remainder decays with slope 2;
- the reduced-model adjoint agrees to 1.3·10⁻¹⁰;
- one gradient costs 30–33 ms for K = 3…90 controls, against 73–1569 ms (forward) and 103–3056 ms (central) for finite differences.

With identical optimisation algorithms on the 18-control open-loop problem, the adjoint optimiser takes 5.6 s and 215 forward + 70 adjoint solves. The finite-difference version takes 23.8 s and 1387 solves (4.3× slower, 4.9× more solves). It also stalls at a KKT residual of 1.5·10⁻², against 4.8·10⁻⁵, because its O(ε) gradient error defeats the line search. The adjoint plan uses 2022 J, 14.6 % less than the original max-penalty optimiser (2369 J) and 45.0 % less than the cheapest safe constant cooling (3679 J).

## 16. Model-predictive control

Every 60 s the twin re-estimates the source and state, re-solves the control problem on a 6 × 100 s horizon, and applies the first segment. A 1 K margin absorbs the known under-estimation of the defect peak. Three MPC optimisers were compared in closed loop on the ground-truth plant over the full charge:

| MPC optimiser | Plant peak | Energy | Optimiser time per decision |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Original (finite differences) | 44.90 °C | 2109 J | 1333 ms |
| Adjoint, full order | 44.93 °C | 2042 J | 755 ms |
| Adjoint, POD ROM r = 40 (certified) | 44.89 °C | 2063 J | 89 ms |

The ROM-based controller optimises on the reduced model with the state constraint restricted to a screened hot region (≈ 300 of 800 cells). It certifies each plan with one full-order solve, applies a defect correction if the full model is violated (needed once in 29 decisions), and rejects the ROM if its peak error exceeds 0.25 K (never triggered; mean error 0.037 K). It never applies an uncertified plan.

## 19. Limitations

- **No experimental validation.** Ground truth is a finer simulation with an exact Gaussian defect. Real cells are 3-D and anisotropic, have temperature-dependent properties and produce electrochemical heat.
- **Model form.** The depth-averaged 2-D model lumps through-thickness gradients into the sink term. The defect is assumed time-invariant in shape and to scale with I².
- **Inverse problem.** The smooth prior halves the reconstructed defect peak (source error ≈ 40 %). Forecasts are biased about 1 K low, which the control margin compensates.
- **Optimisation.** The control problem is non-convex; the optimiser returns local solutions. The Moreau–Yosida treatment leaves O(1/μ) violations that are repaired heuristically by a uniform shift.
- **Reduced order.** No rigorous a-posteriori error bound is computed; safety relies on full-order certification. On a 40×20 grid the full model is already cheap, so ROM gains are modest for plain simulation and large only for optimisation with a screened constraint. The screening is heuristic.
- **Identifiability.** The analysis is local, at the true parameters, for a single defect.
- **Scope.** One scenario family, synthetic data, a single live session.

## 20. Future work

- Adaptive finite elements with goal-oriented (dual-weighted residual) error estimation on unstructured meshes.
- An anisotropic 3-D thermal model with temperature-dependent coefficients (a nonlinear PDE, with Newton–Krylov solvers).
- Bayesian inverse problems and rigorous uncertainty quantification (posterior sampling, Laplace approximation), plus ensemble/Kalman state estimation for time-varying defects.
- Total-variation or L¹ priors for sharper source reconstruction, and optimal sensor placement (A-/D-optimal design on the Fisher information).
- Certified reduced bases with a-posteriori error bounds; hyper-reduction (DEIM) for nonlinear models.
- Multi-cell pack models with distributed optimal control, and validation against measured BMS data.

## 21. Conclusion

ThermoTwin.NET formulates battery thermal safety as a coupled mathematical problem: a parabolic PDE, sparse-data inverse reconstruction, reduced-order simulation and PDE-constrained optimal control. Each component is implemented from first principles in a dependency-free C# library and verified against independent references: exact solutions, a second discretisation, finite differences and full-order certification. The experiments show that:

- careful discretisation and regularisation recover a hidden heat source's location and the full temperature field from twelve sensors;
- an exact discrete adjoint makes PDE-constrained optimisation both faster and more accurate than finite-difference gradients;
- a POD surrogate trained on the right source family makes the control loop an order of magnitude faster without compromising safety on the high-fidelity model.

The principal open step is validation against measured data.
